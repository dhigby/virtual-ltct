    Lesson 1: Supporting the Final Turn¶

    Estimated time: 25 minutes

    Purpose: Before you study any single check, see where your work fits in the whole translation process. You will learn why checks that were "already done" must be run again. You will also learn what your job is, and what it is not, when you support a team.

    Watch the video: To be recorded at stage 8.

    Learning objectives¶

    You will be able to:

    • Describe the translation process as a 6-stage spiral.
    • Explain why Stage 6 (pre-publishing) is the "final turn", when earlier checks are run again until there are zero errors.
    • State your role in a pre-publishing check session: diagnose, coach, and never touch the keyboard.
    • Decide whether an issue is yours to resolve, the team's translation decision, or something to escalate.

    Connect¶

    ✏️ Reflection: Think of a time a team said "we already did that check". Later, that check still had problems. What happened? Did anyone go back and look closely? Or did the team trust the earlier pass and move on?

    Keep that memory in mind. This course comes back to this pattern again and again.

    Content¶

    The 6-stage spiral¶

    A Bible translation project moves through six stages before publication. Think of it as a spiral, not a straight line. Comparison, terms, formatting and other checks come back more than once, each time at a higher level of polish.

    These checks do not run on a fixed schedule at every stage. Some get attention early and again later. Others are missed at one stage and only show up at another.

    The key fact: errors can be introduced, or first appear, at any stage. So a real final check cannot assume that earlier stages caught everything.

    Stage 6: the final turn¶

    Stage 6 is pre-publishing. It is the final turn of the spiral. Anything missed, introduced or left unresolved earlier is still in the text. The typesetter and reviewers will treat this pass as final. So the team runs the earlier checks again. This time they drive them to zero errors.

    Long, high-volume checks still find errors at Stage 6, even when the team "already did" them. The wordlist is the main example. It covers tens of thousands of words, and no single earlier pass catches everything.

    Start with the Project Plan¶

    You already know Paratext's Project Plan from earlier training. It is the "Assignments and Progress" view: a checklist of required tasks for each stage. Each task shows a live status, such as "3 issues" or "No issues".

    At Stage 6, start here. You do not need to rebuild the status by hand. The Project Plan re-runs the basic checks automatically and keeps the status current. You can also rerun any single check directly from there.

    "No issues" can be false-clean¶

    A "No issues" status only shows what the checks can see. Two user overrides can hide errors:

    • denied errors;
    • a blanket-approved status, such as a wordlist where every word is marked Correct.

    An automatic recheck does not undo either override. So the Project Plan can look clean while errors sit underneath.

    This is a false-clean result: an "all clear" that nobody earned. It can also come from a check that was skipped. It is the spine of this course: the one idea that runs through every lesson. Errors get missed or waved through when a team is sure a check is "already done". So "we already did that" is the trap.

    Each check-area lesson shows what false-clean looks like in that tool. For now, learn the pattern: sometimes you must look at the actual results, not only the summary status.

    Your role: diagnose and coach¶

    At Stage 6, your job is not to fix the team's text. You do not speak their language. Your job is to:

    1. Diagnose the cause of what a check finds, not just the symptom.
    2. Explain it in plain words that the team can act on.
    3. Coach them through the fix. The translator makes every correction, at their own keyboard. The team's own workbook teaches the same rule.

    Yours, the team's, or escalate¶

    Not everything you find is yours to resolve. Not everything is the team's decision either. Sort each issue into one of three groups:

    • Yours: a tool or process problem that you can diagnose and coach the team through. Examples: a status set wrongly, a check result the team misunderstood, or glossary links at the wrong scope (over-linked).
    • The team's: a real translation decision. Examples: how a parallel passage should read, or what a rendering should be. You confirm the check ran and the team reviewed it. You do not judge the language content.
    • Escalate: a tool problem you cannot diagnose goes to an LT mentor. A content or translation question that needs a trained eye on Scripture goes to a Translation Consultant.

    Key takeaways

    • Errors can appear at any stage, so Stage 6 re-runs earlier checks to zero errors.
    • Start with the Project Plan, but watch for a false-clean result underneath it.
    • You diagnose and coach. The translator makes every correction.
    • Sort every issue: yours, the team's, or escalate.

    Challenge¶

    ✏️ Try this: Four short exercises. Try each one without looking back. Then check your answers against the Content section above.

    1. Sort these issues. For each one, write "yours", "the team's" or "escalate". If you escalate, name who you go to.
    2. A check status was set wrongly.
    3. The team is not sure how a parallel passage should read.
    4. Glossary links were applied to every occurrence instead of the right scope.
    5. A tool problem that you cannot diagnose.
    6. A content question that needs a trained eye on Scripture.
    7. Explain what "No issues" does not tell you. Write two sentences. In the first, say what the checks have really confirmed. In the second, say what an automatic recheck does not undo. Name both user overrides.
    8. Find it in the Project Plan. Open the Project Plan (the "Assignments and Progress" view) in a project you support. Pick one task and read its status. Then find where you would rerun that one check. You only need to find both things. You are not reviewing the project.
    9. Ask "how", not "whether". Write one question for a team. It should ask how a check status became clear, not whether it is clear. Then write one sentence on why this is the better question. Use the idea of a false-clean result.

    Change¶

    ✏️ Reflection: Think of one team you support now, or will support in future. Where are they most likely to say "we already did that" about a check that still needs a real look? Name the check area if you can.

    Next step: Write down that team and check area. You will use it again in the check-area lessons.
