    Scenario bank¶

    Estimated time: 60 minutes

    Purpose: Seven scenarios that use the whole course. A mentor reviews your answers. These scenarios earn this course's 3 - Independent level (see 00-design.md, "Outcome-level open question").

    • Each core objective (2, 5, 6, 7, 9, 10) has its own scenario.
    • Scenario 7 combines the secondary objectives (3, 4, 8).
    • The false-clean thread (objective 1) runs inside Scenarios 1 and 3.

    Every scenario comes from a confirmed SME field case, a known mistake pattern, or Lessons 1 to 8.

    How to use these scenarios¶

    For learners¶

    1. Read each scenario carefully, including the context.
    2. For each scenario, write three things:
    3. how you would diagnose the situation;
    4. what you would say to the team;
    5. what you would escalate, if anything, and to whom.
    6. Send your written answers to your mentor.

    For mentors¶

    • Use 10-mentor-guide.md. It says what to watch for in each answer. It also gives the "what good looks like" markers from the SME interview.
    • Score reasoning and coaching, not whether the learner names one "correct" fix. Several scenarios have more than one good answer.

    Scenario 1: The Harmonized Synoptics (Objective 2 — parallel passages)¶

    Situation: A team is nearly at Stage 6. Two accounts of the same event have several differences in wording. The team lead says: "Those are supposed to be different. We're not changing them. We checked this months ago."

    Context: You open the Parallel Passages tool and look at the verses side by side. Several verses show a red question mark in the Status column, not a checkmark. This means someone edited those verses in a revision some time ago, and nobody has reviewed them since. You don't speak the team's language, so you can't judge the meaning. But you can see the tool's shading on the Greek and on the team's translation.

    NOTE: A reminder of how the shading works (Lesson 3): - Green shading marks the same three or more words in both passages. - In the Greek, yellow marks words a scholar has marked as equivalent. Yellow counts as shaded. - The shading may look paler in the translation and resources than in the Greek. - Word order can differ, so the shading need not line up word for word. - Unshaded Greek means the originals differ there.

    A mismatch in shading is a question for the team, not an error. The team decides.

    Your task:

    1. Diagnose.
    2. What would you confirm before you accept the team lead's explanation?
    3. How would you use the Status column to check whether these verses were reviewed after the last revision?
    4. How does what you find connect to the "we already did that" trap from Lesson 1?
    5. What could you learn by comparing the shading in the Greek with the shading in the translation?
    6. Say to the team. How would you ask the team to open the Parallel Passages tool and go through each verse that still has a red "?"? Say it without suggesting that you doubt their judgment about real variation.
    7. Escalate. The team's review may show that team members disagree. Some may think a difference is real variation; others may think it is an error. Who handles that: you, the team, or someone else? Say why.

    Scenario 2: The Undocumented Measure (Objective 5 — Numbers and Measures)¶

    Situation: A team asks you to check their Bible for consistent numbers, weights and measures. Here is what happened:

    1. Their Paratext version does not have the new combined list for numbers, weights and measures. It has only the older, separate Numbers list. There is no Measures list. You confirm this, and the team reviews the Numbers list.
    2. You tell the team their version has no list for weights and measures. You coach them to add the measurement terms they need to their project Biblical Terms list, and to review those renderings.
    3. Their review finds two passages that render the same measurement in different ways.
    4. You ask about their documented approach to measures. They aren't sure one exists. Someone remembers "a decision early on", but nobody knows where it is written.

    Context: The new combined list is expected soon, but it does not reach every Paratext version at once. This check area has no well-known field mistake, unlike the wordlist or Biblical Terms. Your job is to confirm which lists this team's version really has. The rest is process and routing, not finding a known error.

    Your task:

    1. Diagnose.
    2. How would you confirm which lists this team's Paratext version has: the new combined list, or only the older Numbers list with no Measures list?
    3. Their version has no list for weights and measures. Why does the team, not you, add the measurement terms and review the renderings?
    4. What would you settle first: the inconsistency itself, or whether an agreed approach exists at all?
    5. What is the difference between "an inconsistency was found" and "the team has no documented standard to check against"?
    6. Say to the team.
    7. How would you explain what their Paratext version has and does not have yet?
    8. How would you help them find their earlier decision, or agree on one now? Do this without suggesting a rendering yourself.
    9. Escalate. Once the approach is written down, who decides which of the two renderings is correct? Could any version of this situation need a Translation Consultant, rather than staying within the team?

    Scenario 3: The Inventory Nobody Opened (Objective 6 — formatting checks)¶

    Situation: A team's Basic Checks show a short list of formatting errors. They are all in the second half of Mark:

    • Several verses in a row, just after one section heading, each have a pair of errors: "Verse number in heading" and "Marker cannot occur here: \v".
    • There is also a reference that looks garbled.

    In that passage, the verses after the heading appear in the heading's bold style. Their verse numbers are missing from the normal text. The team has not mentioned Chapter/Verse Numbers. They went straight to the marker and reference errors.

    The team also says the Punctuation Inventory "was already checked when we started the project." Since then, the project has had two more revisions. It goes to a typesetter in three weeks.

    Context: Time is short. The team wants to hand off soon and thinks both things are already done.

    Your task:

    1. Diagnose.
    2. Before you work through the Basic Checks list from top to bottom, what would you check first, and why?
    3. Chapter/Verse Numbers has not been confirmed clean. Why must you confirm it before anything else, including the marker-pair census?
    4. Suppose you then find a missing \p marker after a section heading in Mark. What would that explain about the other errors, with the verse text swallowed into the heading style? How would you confirm it before you tell the team?
    5. What is wrong with treating "checked when we started the project" as up to date for the Punctuation Inventory?
    6. Say to the team.
    7. What would you say to get them to run Basic Checks again, structural-first?
    8. For the Punctuation Inventory, what exactly would you ask them to open and show you?
    9. Escalate.
    10. The Punctuation Inventory's settings may be really confusing. Or a character you don't recognise may only be a font display problem. Who do you bring in?
    11. If the question is whether a character is valid in the language at all, where does the answer come from?

    Scenario 4: The Every-Occurrence Glossary (Objective 7 — glossary linking)¶

    Situation: You are reviewing a project's glossary links before the final read-through. The team linked the glossary in batches. Each batch's linked-occurrences report was replaced when the next batch ran, so no report is left. You want to check whether "angel", a frequent term in this book, was linked at the correct scope.

    Context: The glossary already exists. Its content is not your job; you are only reviewing the linking.

    Your task:

    1. Diagnose.
    2. There is no report. How would you use Find to check the scope of the "angel" links?
    3. What exactly would you look for in the markup of each Find result? How does it show the difference between over-linking (every occurrence) and correct linking (the first occurrence in each section)?
    4. How could Find's list of results mislead you if you only looked at it quickly?
    5. The glossary also has a phrase term that contains the word "angel", such as "angel of the Lord". A Find result for "angel" may fall inside a marker for that phrase. Is that something to fix, or is it correct? Explain why.
    6. Say to the team.
    7. Describe exactly how you would unlink the term and link it again at the correct scope.
    8. What would you tell the team about why "first occurrence in each section" matters for readers?
    9. Escalate. Is any part of this situation not yours to fix directly? Explain why.

    Scenario 5: The Last Read-Through (Objective 9 — PTXprint draft-PDF read-through)¶

    Situation: You are leading a team through the final PTXprint draft-PDF read-through. After this, the files go to the typesetter. In one sitting you find three problems:

    • one word alone at the top of a column (an orphan word);
    • a footnote that has moved two pages away from its reference;
    • a heading that falls badly at a column break on a spread.

    Context: The team uses PTXprint 3.0.38. They want to sign off and move on. They tend to treat all three problems as "the typesetter's problem now."

    Your task:

    1. Diagnose. For each of the three problems, what do you think is the likely cause? What would you check to confirm it?
    2. Say to the team. Sort the three into "mine to resolve", "the team's decision" and "the typesetter's skill". Explain to the team, in words they can follow, why you are not trying to solve all three yourself.
    3. Escalate. Which of the three, if any, would you pass to the typesetter rather than resolve now? What would you tell the typesetter, so they don't have to find the problem again themselves?

    Scenario 6: The Three Buckets (Objective 10 — triage: yours / team's / escalate)¶

    Situation: In one support session with one team, three things come up:

    1. A check status that was set wrongly. Nobody really reviewed it.
    2. A disagreement in the team about how a passage should read.
    3. A formatting problem in the markup that you cannot diagnose yourself.

    Context: The team wants you to resolve all three before they can continue. To them, the three are the same: "You're the LTC, you sort it out."

    Your task:

    1. Diagnose. Use the three buckets from Lesson 1: yours, the team's, and escalate. Sort each item and explain why. What makes item 1 yours, item 2 the team's, and item 3 an escalation?
    2. Say to the team. In one short conversation, explain why:
    3. you handle one item yourself;
    4. you send the team back to decide another;
    5. you bring in outside help for the third.

    Explain this as a normal division of the work, not as avoiding your job. 3. Escalate. For item 3, who exactly do you bring in: an LT mentor or a Translation Consultant? Why? What would you tell them so they understand the problem quickly?

    Scenario 7 (combined): The Overloaded Setup (Objectives 3, 4, 8 — secondary)¶

    Situation: A team is starting its final formatting and layout work. Three separate things are happening:

    • Biblical Terms. The team says its Biblical Terms list is "done". But several rows still show the blank, highlighted placeholder. One term has several possible renderings, and most of them are no longer found in the current text.
    • Send/Receive. Send/Receive has become much slower over the last month. The team mentions, almost in passing, that they added the full "All Biblical Terms" list to the project. They did this to make consulting terms easier.
    • Layout. The project's expat admin wants a single-column layout. The reason: the language has long words, and the team has never set up hyphenation. But this NT is the first Scripture in the language. So the community has only ever read the Bible in the LWC, and LWC Bibles are normally two-column.

    Context: All three came up in the same conversation. The team sees them as three small requests with no connection.

    Your task:

    1. Diagnose.
    2. Biblical Terms: What would you check to find out whether the list is really "done"? Separately, how would you confirm which of a term's renderings is current and which is an old leftover?
    3. Send/Receive: What is the real cause of the slowdown? Why is it not just "having a big list open"?
    4. Layout: What should decide the layout, rather than word length alone?
    5. Say to the team. For each of the three, write what you would say to the team. Include:
    6. how you would explain the Send/Receive cause to a team that doubts that adding terms and slower Send/Receive are connected;
    7. how you would establish what readers expect from the layout before you agree to any fix.
    8. Escalate.
    9. Which of the three, if any, needs to go beyond you and the team?
    10. Which are fully within your own coaching and technical setup role? Remember: this course's Digital and Print Publishing claim rests on you both advising and doing hands-on setup. That setup includes:
      • turning on Show hyphenation in the Wordlist;
      • drafting breaks from the team's orthography statement, if they can give you one, or entering the breaks the team proposes, if they can't;
      • setting up batch approval;
      • confirming that hyphenation is approved before print.
    11. For hyphenation, say who decides whether each break is right, and how you would get that decision from them.
