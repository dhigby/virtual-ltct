    Lesson 1: Supporting the Final Turn¶

    Estimated time: 35 minutes

    Purpose: Before diving into any single check area, place your work as a consultant inside the shape of the whole translation process — so you know why checks that were "already done" still need re-running, and what your job is (and isn't) when you're in the room.

    Learning objectives¶

    • You will be able to:
    • Describe the translation process as a 6-stage spiral.
    • Explain why Stage 6 (pre-publishing) is the "final turn" that re-runs earlier checks to zero-error.
    • You will be able to:
    • State the consultant's role in a pre-publishing check session — diagnose, coach, never touch the keyboard.
    • Decide when an issue is yours to resolve, the team's translation decision, or something to escalate.

    Connect¶

    ✏️ Reflection: Think about the last time you heard a team say "we already did that check" about something that turned out to still have problems. What happened? Did anyone go back and look closely, or did the team's confidence in an earlier pass carry the issue forward?

    Hold that memory as you go through this lesson — it's the pattern the rest of this course keeps coming back to.

    Content¶

    The 6-stage spiral. A Bible translation project moves through six stages on its way to publication, not a single straight-line pass. The translation progresses through six stages, but can be thought of as a spiral rather than a straight line — the same checks come back around more than once, at increasing levels of polish, rather than each one being done exactly once and left behind. Jenni Beadle's framing (from the SME interview behind this course) is a 6-stage spiral: comparison, terms, formatting, and other checks appear across the process, but not on a fixed schedule of "run at every stage." Some checks get attention early and again later; others get missed at one stage and only surface at another. The key fact to carry forward is that errors can be introduced or first surface at any stage — not only in a late, high-volume pass — which is exactly why a genuine final check can't assume earlier stages already caught everything.

    Stage 6 is the final turn. By the time a project reaches Stage 6 — pre-publishing — whatever has been missed, introduced, or left unresolved at any earlier stage is still sitting in the text, because this pass is what the typesetter and reviewers will treat as final. Long-running, high-volume checks — the wordlist above all — keep surfacing errors at Stage 6 even when the team believes they've "already done" them, simply because a wordlist check spans tens of thousands of words and no single earlier pass reliably catches everything.

    Checking the Project Plan first. You already know Paratext's Project Plan — the "Assignments and Progress" view — from earlier training: a per-stage checklist of required tasks, each showing a live status like "3 issues" or "No issues." It's your starting point at Stage 6, not something you need to reconstruct by hand. The Project Plan automatically re-runs the basic checks and keeps its status current, so you don't need to manually trigger a check just to see where a team stands — you can read it straight off the Project Plan, and rerun any individual check directly from there.

    But the Project Plan can still be false-clean. A "No issues" status only reflects what the check machinery can see — and denied errors or a blanket-approved status are user-level overrides that an automatic recheck doesn't undo. A team can have a Project Plan that looks entirely clean while errors sit underneath it, denied or waved through without review. This is the cross-cutting thread of this whole course. A team's confidence that a check is already handled is exactly the condition under which errors get missed, denied, or waved through unreviewed. You'll meet this pattern by name — a false-clean result — in the check-area lessons that follow, where it shows up as a specific behavior in each check tool (denied errors nobody looked at, a wordlist mass-approved without review, a status blanket-set to "correct"). Right now, just recognize the shape of it: an all-clear that nobody actually earned — and know that means there are times you need to look closer at the actual results, not just trust the Project Plan's summary status.

    Your role: diagnose, coach, never touch the keyboard. When you support a team through Stage 6, your job is not to fix their text. It's to:

    • Diagnose the cause, not just the symptom, of what a check surfaces.
    • Explain it in plain terms the team can act on themselves.
    • Coach them through the fix — the translator makes the actual correction, at their own keyboard, every time. This mirrors the same rule the team's own workbook teaches them.

    Knowing when to escalate. Not everything you find is yours to resolve, and not everything is the team's translation call either. Three buckets:

    • Yours — a tooling or process problem you can diagnose and coach the team through directly (a mis-set status, a misunderstood check result, an over-linked glossary scope).
    • The team's — a genuine translation decision (how a parallel passage should read, what a rendering should be). You confirm the check ran and was reviewed; you don't adjudicate the language content.
    • Escalate — a tooling problem beyond what you can diagnose goes to an LT mentor; a content/translation question that needs a trained eye on Scripture goes to a Translation Consultant.

    Key takeaways - Errors can be introduced or missed at any stage of the 6-stage spiral, not only in a final accumulating pass — that's why Stage 6 can't assume earlier stages caught everything. - The Project Plan gives you a live, current view of check status and lets you rerun a check directly — use it as your starting point, not something to rebuild by hand. - A false-clean result — denied errors, blanket-approved statuses — can occur in any check area, and it can hide underneath an apparently clean Project Plan status; watch for it everywhere, not just once. - Your role is to diagnose and coach, never to make the correction yourself. - Sort every surfaced issue into one of three buckets: yours, the team's, or an escalation.

    Challenge¶

    ✏️ Try this: Four short exercises on this lesson's own material. Write each answer first, then mark it yourself against the Content section above — the point is to find your gaps here, not in front of a team.

    1. From memory, name the three buckets you sort every surfaced issue into, with a one-line definition of each. Then name the two escalation contacts and which kind of problem each one takes. Check yourself against "Knowing when to escalate" — if you had to guess on either contact, that's the line to re-read.
    2. What does a "No issues" status on the Project Plan tell you, and what does it not? Write two sentences: one for what the check machinery has genuinely confirmed, and one for the kind of thing an automatic recheck does not undo. Name both of the user-level overrides this lesson lists.
    3. Open the Project Plan — the "Assignments and Progress" view — in one project you already support. Pick a single task, read its current status straight off the view, and note where on that same view you would rerun that one check from. One narrow action: you're confirming you can find both things, not reviewing a project.
    4. Draft one question — one sentence. It should ask a team how a check status came to be clear, rather than whether it is clear. Say it out loud once and adjust until it sounds like something you'd actually open with. The gap between those two questions is the instinct the rest of this course keeps returning to.

    Change¶

    ✏️ Reflection: Think of one translation team you currently support, or one you support in the future. Where in their process are they most likely to say "we already did that" about a check that still needs a real look? Name the check area if you can.

    Next step: Write down that team and check area — you'll draw on it again as you work through the check-area lessons.
