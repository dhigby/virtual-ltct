# Lesson 1: Supporting the Final Turn

**Estimated time:** 35 minutes

**Purpose:** Before diving into any single check area, place your work as a consultant
inside the shape of the whole translation process — so you know why checks that were
"already done" still need re-running, and what your job is (and isn't) when you're in
the room.

## Learning objectives

- You will be able to describe the translation process as a 6-stage spiral, and explain
  why Stage 6 (pre-publishing) is the "final turn" that re-runs earlier checks to
  zero-error.
- You will be able to state the consultant's role in a pre-publishing check session —
  diagnose, coach, never touch the keyboard — and decide when an issue is yours to
  resolve, the team's translation decision, or something to escalate.

## Connect

**✏️ Reflection:** Think about the last time you heard a team say "we already did that
check" about something that turned out to still have problems. What happened? Did
anyone go back and look closely, or did the team's confidence in an earlier pass carry
the issue forward?

Hold that memory as you go through this lesson — it's the pattern the rest of this
course keeps coming back to.

## Content

**The 6-stage spiral.** A Bible translation project moves through six stages on its way
to publication, not a single straight-line pass. Jenni Beadle's framing (from the SME
interview behind this course) is a **6-stage spiral**: comparison, terms, formatting,
and other checks appear across the process, but not on a fixed schedule of "run at every
stage." Some checks get attention early and again later; others get missed at one stage
and only surface at another. The key fact to carry forward is that **errors can be
introduced or first surface at any stage** — not only in a late, high-volume pass — which
is exactly why a genuine final check can't assume earlier stages already caught
everything.

**Stage 6 is the final turn.** By the time a project reaches Stage 6 — pre-publishing —
whatever has been missed, introduced, or left unresolved at any earlier stage is still
sitting in the text, because this pass is what the typesetter and reviewers will treat as
final. Long-running, high-volume checks — the wordlist above all — keep surfacing errors
at Stage 6 even when the team believes they've "already done" them, simply because a
wordlist check spans tens of thousands of words and no single earlier pass reliably
catches everything.

**Checking the Project Plan first.** You already know Paratext's **Project Plan** — the
"Assignments and Progress" view — from earlier training: a per-stage checklist of
required tasks, each showing a live status like "3 issues" or "No issues." It's your
starting point at Stage 6, not something you need to reconstruct by hand. The Project
Plan automatically re-runs the basic checks and keeps its status current, so you don't
need to manually trigger a check just to see where a team stands — you can read it
straight off the Project Plan, and rerun any individual check directly from there.

**But the Project Plan can still be false-clean.** A "No issues" status only reflects
what the check machinery can see — and denied errors or a blanket-approved status are
user-level overrides that an automatic recheck doesn't undo. A team can have a Project
Plan that looks entirely clean while errors sit underneath it, denied or waved through
without review. This is the cross-cutting thread of this whole course. A team's
confidence that a check is already handled is exactly the condition under which errors
get missed, denied, or waved through unreviewed. You'll meet this pattern by name — a
**false-clean result** — in the check-area lessons that follow, where it shows up as a
specific behavior in each check tool (denied errors nobody looked at, a wordlist
mass-approved without review, a status blanket-set to "correct"). Right now, just
recognize the shape of it: an all-clear that nobody actually earned — and know that
means there are times you need to look closer at the actual results, not just trust the
Project Plan's summary status.

**Your role: diagnose, coach, never touch the keyboard.** When you support a team
through Stage 6, your job is not to fix their text. It's to:

- **Diagnose the cause**, not just the symptom, of what a check surfaces.
- **Explain it in plain terms** the team can act on themselves.
- **Coach them through the fix** — the translator makes the actual correction, at their
  own keyboard, every time. This mirrors the same rule the team's own workbook teaches
  them.

**Knowing when to escalate.** Not everything you find is yours to resolve, and not
everything is the team's translation call either. Three buckets:

- **Yours** — a tooling or process problem you can diagnose and coach the team through
  directly (a mis-set status, a misunderstood check result, an over-linked glossary
  scope).
- **The team's** — a genuine translation decision (how a parallel passage should read,
  what a rendering should be). You confirm the check ran and was reviewed; you don't
  adjudicate the language content.
- **Escalate** — a tooling problem beyond what you can diagnose goes to an **LT mentor**;
  a content/translation question that needs a trained eye on Scripture goes to a
  **Translation Consultant**.

**Key takeaways**
- Errors can be introduced or missed at any stage of the 6-stage spiral, not only in a
  final accumulating pass — that's why Stage 6 can't assume earlier stages caught
  everything.
- The Project Plan gives you a live, current view of check status and lets you rerun a
  check directly — use it as your starting point, not something to rebuild by hand.
- A false-clean result — denied errors, blanket-approved statuses — can occur in any
  check area, and it can hide underneath an apparently clean Project Plan status; watch
  for it everywhere, not just once.
- Your role is to diagnose and coach, never to make the correction yourself.
- Sort every surfaced issue into one of three buckets: yours, the team's, or an
  escalation.

## Challenge

**✏️ Try this:** A team tells you, "We're ready for the typesetter — the Project Plan
shows no issues on every check." Before you believe them:

1. What two or three questions would you ask the team to find out whether that "clean"
   result reflects genuine review, or a status that was cleared without being looked
   at?
2. Pick one check area you expect to be riskiest for a false-clean result on this team,
   and say why.
3. If you did find a denied error or a mass-approved list, what would you say to the
   team to explain what happened and what needs to happen next — without taking over
   their keyboard?

Write out your answers as if you were about to walk into that meeting. You'll use this
same "confirm, don't trust" instinct in every lesson that follows.
